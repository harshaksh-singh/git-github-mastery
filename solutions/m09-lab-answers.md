# Module 9 lab answers: Rebase

Answers to the "Questions" of [the Module 9 labs](../lab-manual/m09-rebase.md). Write your own answers first. Where an answer names a commit ID, it is the ID from the replay (`labs/run ch09/<lab>`); commits you created by hand have other IDs. Section numbers refer to [Chapter 9](../textbook/ch09-rebase.md).

## Lab 9.1: Clean up a messy branch

1. **Why `(2/6)` first.** The first instruction is a `pick` of a commit whose parent already is the commit the rebase builds on. Replaying it would recreate the commit that exists, so Git skips the work: it starts the rebase at that commit and prints no progress line for it. The reflog shows it: the `rebase (start)` entry carries `d33e9b2`, the first commit of the branch, not the tip of `main`. The first instruction that needs a new commit is line 2, the `fixup`.
2. **The two IDs.** A `reword` is done in two steps, both visible in the reflog: the commit is replayed with its old message (`6f5c1b6`, "add test"), then amended with your message, which gives `0a6fabe`. That is the commit the line `[detached HEAD 0a6fabe]` reports. The next instruction, `fixup`, melds "fix test" into it, and melding means creating one more commit that replaces it: `fa94a04`. Only `fa94a04` is in the branch. A commit cannot be changed, so every "change" to one is a new object.
3. **What the diff proves.** `git diff ORIG_HEAD HEAD` compares two snapshots: the tip before the rebase and the tip after it. One deleted line means that regrouping the history changed the final content in one place only, the place you intended (the `drop`). A lost commit would show up as the reversal of its changes, as it does in the failure scenario: `eval/f1.py | 4 ----`. The diff does not prove that each intermediate commit is sound; for that you need `--exec` (section 9.6).
4. **Other handles.** The reflog of the branch (`feat/metrics@{1}` directly after the rebase), the HEAD reflog (the entry below `rebase (start)`), and the commit ID itself if you noted it. `ORIG_HEAD` cannot be trusted once any later command has written it: `git reset`, `git merge`, `git rebase`, `git am`, and therefore also `git pull`. A `git reset` inside an `edit` stop of the same rebase counts (section 9.6).
5. **Removing a commit on purpose.** Write `drop` in front of the line. The check complains about lines that have disappeared, not about commits you explicitly drop.

## Lab 9.2: Transplant a branch with `--onto`

1. **Three roles.** `main`, after `--onto`, is the new base: the copies are built on it. `exp/hybrid-search` is the boundary: commits reachable from it are not replayed, so the range is `exp/hybrid-search..fix/empty-query`. `fix/empty-query` is the branch that gets rewritten; Git switches to it first. The last name may be left out when you are on the branch, which is what the recovery did.
2. **Author and committer.** A replay keeps the author and the author date of the original commit: Asha wrote those changes. The committer is whoever created the new commit object, and that was you, when your rebase ran. "Author somebody else, committer you" on your own branch is the fingerprint of commits you copied.
3. **`HEAD~2` and `HEAD~3`.** After the failure the branch was `main`, then two copies of the experiment, then your two commits. `HEAD~2` is the second experiment copy, the last commit that is not yours, so `HEAD~2..HEAD` is exactly your two commits. With `HEAD~3` as the boundary the range would have three commits and your branch would carry "Experiment: blend BM25 and vector scores". It would apply without a conflict, because it only adds a file, and nothing would warn you.
4. **Why Git could not know.** A branch is a ref to one commit, and Git records no parent branch and no owner of a commit ([Chapter 7](../textbook/ch07-branches.md)). `main..HEAD` is computed from reachability, and Asha's commits are reachable from your branch and not from `main`.
5. **Deleting her branch.** Nothing happens to your branch. Your two commits now descend from `main` and do not refer to her commits. Deleting a branch removes a ref and its reflog, never commits that another ref still reaches.

## Lab 9.3: Autosquash fixups

1. **The connection is text.** The subject of a fixup commit is `fixup! ` followed by the subject of its target. `--autosquash` takes the rest of that line and looks for an earlier commit in the list with that subject (or with that ID). `--fixup=HEAD~2` contributed only this: at commit time Git read the subject of `HEAD~2` and wrote it into the new subject. No ID and no link are stored.
2. **The empty diff.** It proves that the tree at the tip is identical before and after: folding lost nothing and added nothing. It does not prove that each change went into the right commit (look at `git show --stat` of the targets) or that each commit works on its own.
3. **Why one line conflicted.** The fixup commit was made on the tip, where `tests/test_metrics.py` already contains the import and the test that the later commit "Add token-level F1 metric" added. Folding it into "Test exact_match" replays it directly after that commit: base is the tip version of the file, "ours" is the older version without the F1 lines, "theirs" is the tip version with the changed assertion. Relative to the base, one side removed the lines next to the assertion and the other side changed the assertion. Adjacent changes on both sides are a conflict.
4. **Two other recoveries.** (a) Resolve by hand: keep the changed assertion without the F1 lines, `git add`, `git rebase --continue`. Expect a second stop, because the F1 commit is then replayed next to the line you changed. (b) Address the correction to the commit that last touched those lines: undo the fixup commit with `git reset --soft HEAD~1` and commit again with `--fixup=HEAD`, the F1 commit. That folds cleanly; the price is that the test tweak lives in the F1 commit.
5. **The setting.** `rebase.autoSquash` applies to interactive rebases only (section 9.7). A plain `git rebase main` on a branch that is already on `main` reports "up to date" and does nothing. Use `-i`, or type `--autosquash`.

## Lab 9.4: A conflict in a rebase, and who is "ours"

1. **The stages.** Stage 1 (`TOP_K = 5`) is the file in the parent of the commit being replayed, `5ee19f0`. It is the base because a replay asks what the commit changed relative to its parent. Stage 2 (`TOP_K = 8`) is HEAD at the stop, `1c9f69a`: the tip of `main` plus the copy of your first commit. It is "ours" because the result will be committed on top of HEAD. Stage 3 (`TOP_K = 20`) is `REBASE_HEAD`, your commit `569e6c9`: the side being merged in, "theirs".
2. **No warning.** `git restore --ours` takes the whole file from stage 2, including the `return` line that had merged cleanly. After `git add` the index equals HEAD, so there was nothing to commit for this instruction. A replayed commit that turns out empty is dropped by default (`--empty=drop`), because the usual reason is that the change is already upstream (section 9.12).
3. **As a merge.** `git merge main` on `feat/rerank` would stop once, with the markers the other way round: the `HEAD` half and stage 2 hold your value 20, the other half and stage 3 hold 8, and stage 1 is the merge base of the two branches. `--ours` would have been your version.
4. **When `-X theirs` is wrong.** Whenever upstream's side of a conflicting hunk contains something you need. Example: `main` fixed a bug in lines that your branch reformatted. With `-X theirs` your version of the hunk wins, the fix is gone from the result, and the rebase reports success. The option settles textual conflicts in bulk; it does not review them.
5. **Who wrote `ORIG_HEAD`.** First time: `git rebase main` in the Commands part wrote it when that rebase started, and `--continue` does not rewrite it, so it still named `ad106e3`. The reset then set `ORIG_HEAD` to the tip it moved away from, the rebased branch. Second time: the `git rebase main` of the failure scenario wrote it again at its start, and the branch stood at `ad106e3` at that moment. The recovery reset set it to the damaged tip, and `git rebase -X theirs main` set it back to `ad106e3` once more, which is why the verification can diff against it.

## Lab 9.5: Break a branch on purpose and recover it

1. **Still reachable through** `ORIG_HEAD`, the reflog of the branch (`feat/rerank@{1}`), the HEAD reflog (the entries below `rebase (start)`), and the raw commit IDs. No branch and no tag.
2. **Why "wip".** `ORIG_HEAD` is rewritten by every command that moves HEAD drastically. The second careless rebase set it to your three commits; `git reset --hard HEAD~1` then set it to the tip it moved away from, the "wip" commit. That reset was the last writer.
3. **Which reflog first.** The reflog of the branch. Every line is a state the branch was in, with one line per finished rebase, so "which state do I want back" is a short list. The HEAD reflog also holds every step of every rebase and every switch between branches. You need it when the branch reflog is gone, for instance after the branch was deleted, or when the work happened on a detached HEAD.
4. **How long.** With default settings the reflog entries for commits that are no longer reachable from the branch tip expire after 30 days, and the commits are pruned once they are also older than two weeks and maintenance runs (section 9.16). Sooner: `git reflog expire --expire=now --all` followed by `git gc --prune=now` destroys the entries and then the objects. Deleting the branch deletes its reflog at once; the HEAD reflog would still help for a while.
5. **The rescue branch.** A branch protects the commits and gives them a name that survives whatever you type next. You can inspect it with `git log` and `git diff` before touching the real branch. If the reset goes wrong, the anchor is still there. An ID on paper is one typo away from a second incident, and each further `git reset` moves `ORIG_HEAD` again.

## Lab 9.6: Rebase a shared branch and watch a teammate's history duplicate

1. **Ahead 3, behind 3.** The numbers count commits by ID. Her branch has three commits that the new upstream does not reach: the two originals, which upstream replaced with copies under other IDs, and her own. Upstream has three that she does not reach: the README commit and the two copies.
2. **Content was not duplicated.** The merge output lists one changed file, `README.md | 3 +++`. Everything else was identical on both sides, since the copies make the same changes as the originals. Only the history contains the changes twice.
3. **The `git cherry` output.** `-` in front of "Add document loader" and "Add text cleaner": these commits of her old branch have a counterpart with the same patch ID in the new upstream, so they must not be replayed. `+` in front of "Add chunker": no counterpart, it is hers alone. The repair is to replay the `+` commits, and only those, onto the new upstream.
4. **The boundary.** `dc5df93` was the tip of the upstream branch when she built her commit on it; everything after it is hers. Ways to find it: `git merge-base --fork-point origin/feat/ingest feat/ingest`; `git rev-parse "origin/feat/ingest@{1}"` after the fetch; the line `+ dc5df93...8aca274` that the fetch printed; or counting, since she has one commit of her own: `feat/ingest~1`. A plain `git pull --rebase` uses the first method by itself.
5. **If she had pushed the merge.** The server's branch would be her merge commit, and through its two parents both the copies and the originals: the commits you removed are back. Your next pull would fast-forward your branch to her merge, and you would have the duplicated history too. Removing it would take another rewrite of the shared branch.
6. **Before and after.** Before: ask who has work on the branch. Unpushed commits in other clones are invisible to you, so asking is the only way. Agree on a moment, or merge `main` into the branch and do not rebase. After: tell everyone that the branch was rewritten, from which tip to which tip, and give the command: `git pull --rebase`, or `git rebase --onto origin/feat/ingest dc5df93`. And: do not merge.

## Lab 9.7: `--update-refs` on stacked branches

1. **Position of the lines.** Each `update-ref` line stands directly after the last instruction that belongs to that branch: after "Close files after loading" and the `fixup` that `--autosquash` moved below it, and after "Add text cleaner". When the rebase reaches such a line it notes the current HEAD for that ref. Because the `fixup` comes first, `feat/ingest-loader` ends on the corrected commit. Had the lines been in the other order, the bottom branch would point at the uncorrected commit.
2. **All three.** The corrected commit is the tip of `feat/ingest-loader` and an ancestor of the two branches above it.
3. **One out of three.** `git reset --hard` moves the current branch and nothing else, and `ORIG_HEAD` recorded the old tip of that branch only. The other two were moved by their `update-ref` lines; their old positions are in their own reflogs, one entry down from "rewritten during rebase".
4. **A second worktree.** `--update-refs` leaves a branch alone that is checked out in another worktree (the manual says so), and does not list it among the updated refs, so the stack is half moved after the rebase. `git branch -f` refuses with a fatal error, because the branch is in use by that worktree. Fix it from inside that worktree, with `git reset --hard <commit>` there.
5. **Publishing.** All three branches were rewritten, so three refs on the server need a forced update, each with its own lease: `git push --force-with-lease --force-if-includes origin feat/ingest-loader feat/ingest-cleaner feat/ingest-chunker` (section 9.9).
